Every organization's AI journey looks different, but the path to lasting value follows a recognizable pattern. First you decide where AI should create value and how to govern it. Then you build and integrate solutions into real workflows. Finally, you run, improve and expand them over time.

At NeuSix we describe this as three stages: **Advisory**, **Development and Integration**, and **Managed Services**. Understanding which stage you are in, for each part of your business, helps you invest in the right things and avoid the most common traps.

## Stage 1: Advisory

The advisory stage is about direction. Its goal is to decide where AI should create value, what it will take and how it will be governed.

Typical work in this stage includes:

- **Strategy and opportunity mapping.** Identifying and prioritizing use cases based on value, feasibility and risk.
- **Use cases and workflow or agent design.** Defining how AI will fit into real processes and decisions, including where people stay in the loop.
- **Governance and value measurement.** Setting the guardrails, ownership and metrics that will guide delivery.

**Signs you are in this stage:** you have many ideas but no clear priorities, pilots are scattered across teams, or leaders are asking for an AI strategy.

**Common trap:** treating advisory as a report rather than a decision. A good advisory stage ends with a prioritized roadmap, funded initiatives and agreed governance.

## Stage 2: Development and Integration

The second stage turns priorities into working solutions. Its goal is to build AI capabilities that run inside the business, connected to real data and systems.

Typical work includes:

- **Architecture and orchestration.** Designing how models, data, tools and applications work together, securely and at scale.
- **Retrieval and data enablement.** Preparing the data foundations AI needs, including retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for knowledge-heavy use cases.
- **Agent build and integration.** Building AI agents and services and integrating them with core systems such as ERP, CRM and case management.

**Signs you are in this stage:** priorities are clear, funding is in place, and the challenge is execution: data access, integration, security and adoption.

**Common trap:** building in isolation. Solutions created outside the workflow, without the people who will use them, rarely reach adoption. Co-creation with users and early attention to production requirements are essential. Our article on [why enterprise AI pilots fail](/blog/why-enterprise-ai-pilots-fail) covers this in detail.

## Stage 3: Managed Services

The third stage is about sustaining and growing value. AI systems are not finished when they launch. Models drift, data changes, users find new needs and new opportunities appear.

Typical work includes:

- Monitoring performance, reliability, cost and risk.
- Continuous improvement based on feedback and results.
- Support and incident management with clear service levels.
- Expanding proven solutions to adjacent use cases and teams.

**Signs you are in this stage:** you have AI in production and need it to stay reliable, compliant and valuable while your teams focus elsewhere.

**Common trap:** assuming launch is the finish line. Without ongoing ownership, the value of AI systems quietly decays.

## Different parts of the business, different stages

Maturity is rarely uniform. Customer service may already run AI in production while finance is still exploring use cases. That is normal. Map maturity by business area, then plan support accordingly. An [AI Center of Excellence](/blog/how-to-build-an-ai-center-of-excellence) can help coordinate across areas and share what works.

## Matching support to each stage

| Stage | Main question | What good support looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Advisory | Where should AI create value? | Strategy, prioritization, governance and value metrics |
| Development and Integration | How do we build it into the business? | Architecture, data, engineering, integration and adoption |
| Managed Services | How do we keep it valuable? | Monitoring, support, improvement and expansion |

Commercial models can follow the same logic. Some organizations want a partner to build and hand over. Others want a partner to build, run and then transfer, or to run solutions long term. Our guide to [B&T, BOT and BOM models](/blog/bt-bot-bom-commercial-models-ai-delivery) explains the options.

## Moving between stages

The transitions are where value is most often lost. To move well:

- **From Advisory to Development:** turn the roadmap into funded initiatives with owners, success measures and a delivery plan.
- **From Development to Managed Services:** agree service levels, monitoring, support processes and who owns improvement before launch.
- **From Managed Services back to Advisory:** use results from production to refresh priorities and identify the next wave of opportunities.

The stages form a cycle, not a line. Mature organizations keep moving through them as their ambitions grow.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an AI maturity model?

An AI maturity model describes the stages organizations move through as they adopt AI, from early exploration to running AI at scale. It helps leaders understand where they are and what to focus on next.

### Can we skip the advisory stage?

You can, but it is risky. Without clear priorities and governance, organizations tend to accumulate disconnected pilots that are hard to scale. Even a short, focused advisory phase pays for itself.

### When should we consider AI managed services?

When AI systems are in production and you need them to stay reliable, compliant and improving, but you would rather your internal teams focus on new initiatives or core work.

### How long does each stage take?

It depends on scope. A focused advisory phase may take weeks, building a solution may take a few months, and managed services are ongoing. Each stage should deliver visible value on its own.
