Over the past few years, enterprises have built impressive AI competence. They have hired data scientists, set up platforms, run hackathons and trained thousands of employees. Many can build a capable AI solution in weeks.

Yet many leaders still struggle to point to AI changing the numbers that matter. The reason is a gap between **AI competence** and **AI relevance**.

## Defining the two

**AI competence** is the ability to build and operate AI: skills, tools, platforms, data and engineering practices. It answers the question, "Can we do AI well?"

**AI relevance** is the degree to which AI improves the outcomes your organization cares about: revenue, cost, risk, quality, speed and customer and employee experience. It answers the question, "Does AI make our business better?"

Competence is necessary. It is not sufficient. A highly competent team can still spend years building solutions that nobody uses or that change nothing important.

## Why the gap appears

### Technology-first priorities

When AI programs start from what the technology can do, they produce interesting solutions in search of a problem. Relevance requires starting from what the business needs.

### Success measured by activity

Counting models built, use cases explored or employees trained measures competence. It says little about whether anything improved.

### Solutions outside the workflow

AI that lives in a separate tool asks people to change how they work before they trust it. Without integration into everyday systems and decisions, adoption stays low.

### No ownership of adoption

Technical teams can build and launch. Only business leaders can change processes, incentives and habits so that AI is actually used.

## Five ways leaders can build AI relevance

### 1. Start from decisions and workflows

Ask where better decisions, faster processes or fewer errors would make a real difference. Then ask how AI could help. This keeps investment anchored to value. Our [Discover, Plan, Build, Scale](/blog/discover-plan-build-scale-execution-systems) approach starts here for exactly this reason.

### 2. Make outcomes the unit of priority

Prioritize initiatives by expected business impact, feasibility and risk. Agree a baseline and a target for each. Fund outcomes, not experiments.

### 3. Integrate where the work happens

Deliver AI inside the systems people already use. Integration and change management often take more effort than the model itself, and they are where most of the value is unlocked.

### 4. Make business leaders accountable for adoption

Every AI initiative should have a business owner responsible for adoption and results. Technology teams build; business teams change how work gets done.

### 5. Measure, learn and expand

Track outcomes after launch, not just delivery milestones. When a solution works, expand it to adjacent teams and processes. When it does not, stop or reshape it. Relevance compounds when successful patterns spread.

## Where relevance usually shows up first

Relevance tends to appear fastest where three conditions meet: a frequent, repeatable decision or task, data that already exists in usable form, and a business owner who feels the pain today. Common examples include:

- **Service operations**, where AI helps agents find answers, summarize cases and route requests.
- **Document-heavy processes**, such as contracts, claims, onboarding and compliance reviews.
- **Planning and forecasting**, where better predictions improve inventory, staffing or capacity decisions.
- **Engineering and IT operations**, where AI speeds up testing, incident triage and modernization work.

Starting in areas like these builds credibility, funding and know-how for more ambitious initiatives later. You can see examples across sectors in our [case studies](/our-work/case-studies).

## Questions to test your AI relevance

- Can we name the business outcomes AI has improved in the last year, with numbers?
- Does each AI initiative have a business owner accountable for adoption?
- Are our AI solutions used inside everyday workflows?
- Do we stop initiatives that do not deliver?
- Is our AI roadmap driven by business priorities or by technology possibilities?

If the answers are uncertain, the gap is likely between competence and relevance.

## Competence still matters

None of this means competence is unimportant. Without strong skills, platforms, data and governance, relevance cannot be sustained. The point is balance. Competence should be built in service of relevance, guided by the outcomes the business needs. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is AI relevance?

AI relevance is the extent to which AI improves the business outcomes an organization cares about, such as revenue, cost, risk, quality and experience, rather than simply demonstrating technical capability.

### How do we measure the business value of AI?

Agree a baseline and a target for each initiative before you build, measure the outcome after launch and compare. Combine business metrics with adoption measures, because unused solutions cannot create value.

### Who should own AI relevance in an organization?

Senior business leaders, supported by technology and data leaders. Relevance depends on decisions about priorities, processes and adoption that only the business can make.

### Is it better to focus on a few AI initiatives or many?

Usually a few, done well. A small number of initiatives tied to important outcomes, fully integrated and adopted, creates more value and learning than many disconnected pilots.

## Leading for relevance

The organizations that win with AI will not necessarily be those with the most advanced models. They will be those that consistently connect AI to the outcomes that matter. That is a leadership task as much as a technical one.
