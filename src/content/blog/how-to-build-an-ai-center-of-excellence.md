Many enterprises set up an AI Center of Excellence (CoE) with high expectations. A year later, some have become the engine of AI adoption across the organization. Others have become a queue: a central team that every business unit must wait for, with growing frustration on both sides.

The difference is rarely talent or budget. It is design. This guide explains how to build an AI CoE that accelerates delivery rather than slowing it down.

## What an AI CoE is for

An AI Center of Excellence is a cross-functional team that sets direction, standards and shared capabilities for AI across an organization. Its job is to make it easier, faster and safer for the whole business to create value with AI.

That usually means five responsibilities:

1. **Strategy and prioritization.** Helping leadership decide where AI should create value.
2. **Governance.** Setting the policies, risk tiers and controls that keep AI responsible and compliant.
3. **Platforms and patterns.** Providing shared tools, architectures and reusable components.
4. **Delivery support.** Helping business teams design, build and scale solutions.
5. **Skills and community.** Building AI literacy and connecting practitioners across the business.

What the CoE should *not* be is the only place AI is allowed to happen.

## Choosing an operating model

### Centralized

A single central team designs and delivers all AI work. This gives strong control and consistency, and suits early stages when skills are scarce. Over time it tends to become a bottleneck.

### Federated

Business units build their own AI capabilities with light central coordination. This is fast and close to the business, but often leads to duplicated effort, inconsistent standards and uneven risk management.

### Hub and spoke

A central hub owns strategy, governance, platforms and specialist expertise. Spokes in business units own use cases and delivery, supported by the hub. For most enterprises, this hybrid is the best long-term model. Many start more centralized and shift toward hub and spoke as capability grows.

## Building the CoE step by step

### 1. Define the charter

Write down the CoE's mandate, scope, decision rights and success measures. Agree who it reports to and how it works with IT, data, security, risk and business units. A clear charter prevents turf battles later.

### 2. Secure executive sponsorship

A CoE needs a senior sponsor who can align priorities across functions and protect funding. Sponsorship should come with expectations: the CoE must show value within a reasonable time.

### 3. Start small with the right people

A strong core team typically combines AI and data expertise, engineering and architecture, product thinking, and governance or risk skills. Add business translators who understand processes and can turn needs into well-defined use cases.

### 4. Deliver early, visible wins

Pick a small number of high-value, achievable initiatives and deliver them well. Early results build credibility and generate the reusable assets that make later work faster. Follow a clear execution path such as [Discover, Plan, Build, Scale](/blog/discover-plan-build-scale-execution-systems).

### 5. Build shared platforms and patterns

The biggest multiplier a CoE can offer is reuse. Standard architectures, approved models, retrieval and data pipelines, evaluation tools, security patterns and templates let business teams move much faster with lower risk.

### 6. Put governance into practice

Translate AI principles into a working process: an inventory of AI systems, risk tiers, review steps and monitoring requirements. Keep it proportionate so low-risk work moves quickly. See our guide to [responsible AI governance for production systems](/blog/responsible-ai-governance-production).

### 7. Grow skills across the business

Run training for leaders, practitioners and end users. Create communities of practice where teams share lessons. The aim is to make AI capability widespread, not concentrated.

### 8. Measure what matters

Track business outcomes delivered, the number of solutions in production, adoption, time from idea to production and reuse of shared assets. Avoid measuring activity alone, such as the number of use cases explored.

## Common reasons CoEs fail

- **No clear mandate**, so the CoE competes with IT or business units instead of enabling them.
- **Too centralized for too long**, creating a queue of frustrated stakeholders.
- **Research focus without delivery**, producing interesting work that never reaches production.
- **Governance as gatekeeping**, adding delay without adding confidence.
- **No business ownership**, so solutions launch but are not adopted.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does an AI Center of Excellence do?

It sets AI strategy and standards, governs risk, provides shared platforms and expertise, supports delivery teams and builds AI skills across the organization.

### How big should an AI CoE be?

Start small, with a core team that covers AI, engineering, product and governance. Grow based on demand and results. In a hub-and-spoke model, much of the capacity eventually sits in business units rather than the hub.

### Who should lead an AI CoE?

A leader who combines technical credibility with business influence. The role often reports to a chief data, digital, technology or transformation officer, with strong links to business leadership.

### How long does it take to set up an AI CoE?

A focused CoE can be chartered and staffed and start delivering its first initiatives within a few months. Building a mature hub-and-spoke model with shared platforms takes longer and should be planned in stages.
