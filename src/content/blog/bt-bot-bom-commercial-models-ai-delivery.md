When organizations plan an AI initiative, most of the attention goes to use cases, data and technology. The commercial model gets less thought, yet it shapes who owns the result, who carries the risk and how quickly your own team becomes self-sufficient.

NeuSix offers three commercial models for AI delivery: **Build and Transfer (B&T)**, **Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT)** and **Build-Operate-Maintain (BOM)**. Each is a valid choice. The right one depends on your circumstances.

## The three models at a glance

### Build and Transfer (B&T)

NeuSix builds the solution and hands it over to your team with documentation, enablement and transition support. Your team operates it from launch onwards.

### Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT)

NeuSix builds and operates the solution for an agreed period, then transfers it to your team once it is stable and adoption is proven.

### Build-Operate-Maintain (BOM)

NeuSix builds and continues to operate and maintain the solution long term, with clear SLAs, governance and continuous improvement.

## How they compare

| | B&T | BOT | BOM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who operates after launch | Your team | NeuSix, then your team | NeuSix |
| Time to full ownership | Shortest | After an agreed period | Ownership of outcomes, partner runs operations |
| Internal capability needed early | High | Medium, growing over time | Lower |
| Risk during early operation | Mostly yours | Shared, then yours | Largely with the partner under SLAs |
| Best for | Mature teams, clear scope | Building capability while reducing risk | Critical systems, limited internal capacity |

## When B&T is the right choice

B&T works best when you already have strong engineering and operations teams who can absorb a new system quickly. It is also a good fit when the scope is well defined and the solution resembles systems your team already runs.

The key to success is the transfer itself. Insist on clear documentation, architecture decision records, runbooks, test suites and hands-on enablement sessions. Plan for your team to work alongside the build team before handover, not only after it.

## When BOT is the right choice

BOT is often the best balance for organizations that want to own their AI capability but are not ready to run it from day one. The partner carries the operational load while the solution stabilizes, users adopt it and issues are resolved. Meanwhile, your team builds the skills and processes to take over.

Successful BOT engagements agree three things up front:

1. **Transfer criteria.** Stability, performance, adoption and documentation thresholds that signal readiness.
2. **A capability plan.** Who on your side will take over, and how they will be trained and involved along the way.
3. **A transition timeline.** Clear phases, with shared operation before full handover.

BOT also works for teams, not only solutions. An offshore delivery capability can be built, operated and transferred in the same way. See our article on [delivery pods and the ODC model](/blog/odc-delivery-pods-vs-staff-augmentation).

## When BOM is the right choice

BOM suits solutions that need dependable, long-term operation and continuous improvement, especially when your internal teams are focused elsewhere. It is common for business-critical systems, solutions that need specialist skills to run well, or portfolios of AI capabilities that benefit from a single operating model.

Strong BOM arrangements include clear SLAs, transparent reporting, governance forums, security and compliance commitments, and a plan for continuous improvement, not just maintenance. You should also retain ownership of your data, models and intellectual property as agreed, and have a defined exit path.

## Factors that decide the right model

The right model depends on your internal capability maturity, timeline urgency and governance needs. In practice, consider:

- **Capability maturity.** Do you have people who can operate, monitor and improve AI systems today?
- **Timeline urgency.** Do you need value quickly, before your team can be ready to run the solution?
- **Governance needs.** How critical and regulated is the system, and what assurance do you need during operation?
- **Strategic importance.** Is this capability core to your competitive advantage, which argues for eventual ownership?
- **Budget profile.** Do you prefer project-based investment or ongoing operational spend?

## Combining models

Many organizations use more than one model. A core platform may be delivered through BOT so the internal team eventually owns it, while a set of supporting automations runs under BOM. Models can also change over time: a BOM engagement can become a transfer when your team is ready.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does Build-Operate-Transfer mean?

Build-Operate-Transfer is a model in which a partner builds a solution or team, operates it for an agreed period until it is stable and proven, and then transfers ownership and operation to the client.

### Is BOT more expensive than B&T?

BOT includes a period of operation, so the total engagement is usually larger. It also reduces the risk of a difficult handover and gives your team time to build skills, which can lower the overall cost of ownership.

### Can we switch from BOM to ownership later?

Yes, if the agreement includes a transfer path. Good BOM contracts define documentation standards, knowledge sharing and exit terms from the start.

### Which model is best for a first AI initiative?

Many organizations choose BOT for their first significant initiative. It balances speed and risk while building the internal capability needed for future projects.

## Choosing with confidence

There is no single best model. There is a best model for your capability, timeline and governance needs today, and it may change as you mature. Making the choice deliberately, rather than by default, protects both value and independence.
